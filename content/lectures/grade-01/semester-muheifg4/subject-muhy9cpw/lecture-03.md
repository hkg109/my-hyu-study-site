### Slides 30–41 · collaboration is an organizational capability

#### Collaboration performance model

`Performance = culture/structure/reward × fit-for-work tools × clear roles and quality criteria`

- **Human teamwork:** agree on goal, role, decision right, conflict resolution, and success measure.
- **Human-agent teamwork:** document workflow, human handoff, review criteria, and change authority.
- **Tool fit:** start from the same/different time × same/different place matrix.
- **Governance:** identity, permission, retention, external sharing, data location, AI source, human review.
- **Measurement:** favor search success, cycle time, quality, experience, and business outcome over login count.

> [!CAUTION]
> More messages do not prove better collaboration. Overload, politics, and unclear responsibility can increase conversation while lowering decision quality.

#### Add sustainability to the investment test

Write `electricity, carbon, water, cooling, hardware, supply chain` beside cloud/AI benefits. Unit efficiency and total consumption must be evaluated together.

#### How culture, tools, and outcomes connect

| Concept | Center of attention | Intended result | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Collaboration | Coordinating toward a shared goal | Productivity, quality, innovation, service | Treating message volume as success |
| Social business | Engaging employees, customers, and suppliers through social platforms | Faster decisions and transparent exchange | Assuming a platform creates an open culture |

`shared goal → supportive structure and culture → suitable tools → participation and knowledge sharing → measurable work outcome`

- **Structure:** permit cross-boundary teams and give them decision rights.
- **Culture:** make it safe to share mistakes and know-how.
- **Leadership:** use team input and reward useful participation.
- **Technology:** match channel and workspace capabilities to time, place, and task.

> [!CAUTION]
> Figure 2.7 presents benefits as conditional. In a politically conflicted or command-and-control setting, a new platform may only transmit existing problems faster.

| Human-agent control question | What to document |
| --- | --- |
| What may the agent execute? | Permitted data, tools, and actions |
| When does it hand off? | Risk, uncertainty, value, and stop thresholds |
| Who reviews the result? | Reviewer, quality test, and approval record |
| Who may change the workflow? | Change authority, testing, release, and rollback |

Final slide 32 expands collaboration to ==coordination among people, data, applications, and agents==. Effective users share failures and quality standards, not just successful prompts.

**Tool-selection sequence:** `problem → time/place pattern → solution types → cost/benefit → security and governance → user implementation → vendor and outcome evaluation`.

Prefer active use, search success, cycle time, quality, employee experience, and business outcomes over login counts. For AI/cloud services, also examine energy, carbon, water, cooling, hardware, and supply-chain effects—both efficiency per unit and total use.

### Slides 42–53 · turn data and experience into usable knowledge

#### Concept ladder

| Stage | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data | Raw observation | 120 customer contacts |
| Information | Organized for a purpose | 45% concern late delivery |
| Knowledge | Cause, rule, and context understood | A regional stock-sync delay causes the pattern |
| Wisdom | Judgment about when/how to act | Separate immediate repair from system redesign |

- **Explicit knowledge:** expressed and stored in rules, reports, and documents.
- **Tacit knowledge:** embodied know-how that cannot be fully written down.
- **Stickiness:** difficulty moving knowledge away from its people, culture, and setting.
- **Situational character:** the same procedure works differently across conditions.

#### How knowledge becomes valuable

`Acquire/capture → store with owner/metadata/access → disseminate/search/train → apply → feedback`

> [!EXAM]
> Generative AI accelerates stages but does not create trust. Source, version, ownership, permission, retention, quality, and human review prevent a fast answer from becoming a fast-spreading error.

#### Each stage of the knowledge value chain

Knowledge is not merely a file; it is a **contextual judgment resource** the organization can reuse.

| Property | Meaning | Management implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intangible | Hard to observe directly | Measure use and outcomes |
| Social | Formed through language and relationships | Support mentoring and communities of practice |
| Sticky | Bound to people, place, and culture | Transfer context, not only documents |
| Conditional | Effect changes by situation | Record exceptions and conditions of use |
| Network effect | Sharing and validation can increase value | Combine access with quality control |

`observe and measure → experiment and receive feedback → organize knowledge → change action → measure again`

| Stage | Main work | Necessary control | Generative-AI support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acquire | Capture documents, tacit know-how, TPS/external data | Purpose, consent, provenance | Transcription and classification |
| Store | Manage repositories, schemas, versions, owners | Access, retention, freshness | Duplicate detection and metadata suggestions |
| Disseminate | Search, portals, wikis, training, expert links | Overload and confidentiality | Contextual retrieval from approved sources |
| Apply | Embed knowledge in decisions and work | Human approval and exception paths | Drafting, recommendation, workflow assistance |
| Learn | Feed use and errors back into the system | Monitoring and escalation | Pattern summaries |

> [!EXAM]
> The chain ends with **application and feedback**, not storage. Faster retrieval without changed behavior is not completed organizational learning.

CKOs and knowledge managers supply ownership and quality management. **Communities of practice** transfer experience informally and shorten learning curves; incentives and work time make contribution sustainable.

### Slides 54–67 · choose the correct knowledge-system family

#### Three-family comparison

| Family | User | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise KMS | Whole organization | Collect, classify, retrieve, share, apply | CMS, expert directory, LMS |
| KWS | Knowledge worker | Produce new design, analysis, knowledge | CAD, VR/AR, investment workstation |
| Intelligent technique | Analyst, worker, system | Discover pattern, preserve expertise, solve, automate | Expert system, ML, RAG, agent |

#### AI-ready content checklist · Q-M-A-P-E

- **Quality:** deduplicate, retire stale content, name authoritative versions.
- **Metadata:** category, date, sensitivity, context, retention.
- **Access:** preserve source permissions and legal restrictions.
- **Provenance:** source, authoring date, history, correction path.
- **Evaluation:** retrieval coverage, accuracy, confidentiality, bias, real-work performance.

> [!DEFINITION]
> **RAG** retrieves approved enterprise sources to give a model relevant context. It is not safe when its source content or access control is unsafe.

**Responsible-AI chain:** `govern → map → measure → manage`.

#### Roles of content, knowledge workers, and intelligent techniques

| Target | Problem solved | Representative capability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise content | Make distributed material findable and reusable | CMS/ECM, digital assets, expert directories, LMS |
| Knowledge workers | Produce new designs, models, and expertise | CAD, 3D printing, VR/AR/MR, investment workstations |
| Repeated judgment and patterns | Preserve expertise, classify, predict, search, automate | Expert systems, ML, RAG, agents |

| Content form | Examples | Management need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structured | Rules, formal reports, database records | Schema and validation |
| Semistructured | Email, slides, video | Metadata, tags, taxonomy |
| Unstructured or tacit | Free text, experience, know-how | Search, interview, expert links, context |

The often-cited 80% semistructured/unstructured share is not universal; measure actual holdings and use. Figure 11.8 shows users supplying, governing, and retrieving content.

**Knowledge-worker division of labor**

- The worker maintains expertise, creates new knowledge, advises the firm, and judges validity.
- The KWS supplies computation, graphics, simulation, external sources, documents, communication, and task-specific interfaces.
- The tool does not inherit the professional's accountability for applying the result.

**Technique progression:** `expert systems → case-based reasoning and fuzzy logic → neural networks, data mining, genetic algorithms → ML, NLP, generative AI, RAG, knowledge graphs, policy-controlled agents`.

> [!CAUTION]
> Newer is not automatically more suitable. Match the method to problem structure, explainability, data quality, error cost, and human oversight. **AI output is a claim to verify, not authoritative organizational knowledge.**

#### Role map

| --- | --- |

| CISO/CSO | Security risk, controls, incident response |
| CKO | Knowledge acquisition, sharing, application |

**Modern operating model:** central teams keep architecture, security, shared platforms, data standards, vendors, and reliability coherent; distributed product teams own outcomes, priorities, process improvement, and adoption close to the work.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Why is an AI output not automatically organizational knowledge?
> A: Its source, context, accuracy, access, currency, and application conditions require verification and accountable approval.

> [!QUIZ]
> Name three controls shared by cloud ERP, human-agent teamwork, and RAG-based knowledge search.

> [!ANSWER]
> Examples: **defined authority and accountability**, **quality/security/access control**, and **monitoring, human review, and exception handling**.

#### How the IS function and business teams share responsibility

The IS department is the formal technology-service unit, commonly led by a CIO and joined by CISO/CSO, CKO, CPO, CDO, and increasingly CAIO or responsible-AI leadership. Programmers build code; systems analysts translate business problems into information requirements and system specifications; IS managers coordinate people and services. Size and structure vary by organization.

End users are business-department participants for whom applications are built and whose role in design has grown. **IT governance** defines strategy, policy, decision rights, and accountability so technology supports organizational goals. A modern operating model keeps enterprise architecture, cybersecurity, shared platforms, data standards, vendors, reliability, and organization-wide governance central, while product teams distributed through the business define outcomes, prioritize work, improve processes, drive adoption, and share responsibility for data and AI.

Career paths include business systems analysis, product ownership, BI/data analysis, security/privacy, enterprise/cloud consulting, AI process analysis, and information/knowledge management. The deck's current context expects humans and technology to share changing task mixes; the transferable advantage is process redesign, evidence evaluation, stakeholder communication, data literacy, and responsible AI use. The final learning check asks students to distinguish AI-enabled redesign from simple automation, identify cloud-ERP risks, specify human-agent controls, prepare content for RAG, and include environmental measures in an IS investment decision.

## 1. A conversation is not yet collaboration

Our invented café chain has a group chat. Staff send many messages, but branches keep repeating the same ordering mistakes. The missing element may be a shared goal, a way of making decisions, or a place to preserve useful conclusions.

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Collaboration** means working together toward shared, explicit goals. **Social business** uses internal and external social networking platforms to engage employees, customers, or suppliers in business activity.

A chat channel can support collaboration, but message volume is not its purpose. Social business can help people locate expertise, receive feedback, or solve customer problems; merely maintaining a public social-media account does not demonstrate effective collaboration.

The textbook connects growing collaboration needs to changing work, more interaction-based jobs, decentralized decisions, geographically dispersed operations, innovation, and expectations about work. Tasks increasingly require knowledge that no single worker possesses.

| Intended benefit | Mechanism | More useful evidence than activity counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Productivity | Avoid duplicated work and repeated searching | Time to resolve a recurring task |
| Quality | Bring relevant expertise into a decision | Fewer repeat errors |
| Innovation | Combine different perspectives | Useful ideas tested and adopted |
| Customer service | Share context across employees | Resolution time and repeat complaints |

**Culture connects the tool to the result.** Managers must support knowledge sharing, give people time to contribute, and avoid incentives that reward hoarding information. This is Chapter 1's complementary-assets argument in a specific setting.

> [!QUIZ]
> 2B Q1. A manager buys a collaboration platform but rewards only individual output and discourages asking colleagues for help. Why might adoption fail?

> [!ANSWER]
> The technology supports interaction while the incentive system discourages it. Employees have little reason to contribute or ask questions. Shared goals, managerial support, time, and appropriate rewards are complementary assets; installing software does not supply them automatically.

## 2. Choose tools by time and place

Read **Figure 2.8, the time/space matrix**, 18th book p.56 / PDF 85. Recreate the two axes before memorizing any examples.

| | Same place | Different places |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same time · synchronous | Face-to-face discussion with a shared display | Video meeting, live shared editing |
| Different times · asynchronous | A shift-handoff workspace used at different times | Wiki, discussion thread, versioned document |

The café's morning and evening shifts can be **same place, different time**. A Seoul branch and a Busan branch in a live call are **different place, same time**. Physical distance and simultaneous participation are independent dimensions. One platform may support several cells.

### Read the tool categories as capabilities

- **Email and messaging:** exchange requests and updates; decide which channel carries an authoritative decision.
- **Meeting and collaboration platforms:** combine conversation, shared files, and team workspaces; define ownership and access.
- **Enterprise social networks:** profiles, feeds, groups, content sharing, permissions, and tagging make expertise discoverable.
- **Virtual worlds and metaverse tools:** avatars and shared environments can support interaction; evaluate whether immersion helps the task.
- **AI assistance:** meeting summaries, translation, retrieval, and drafting may reduce routine effort; important outputs need checking against their sources.

Product examples in the textbook illustrate categories at the time of writing. Learn the capabilities and conditions, not a vendor popularity ranking.

### A six-step selection route

The sequence below paraphrases the book's evaluation process (18th book p.56).

1. Locate actual collaboration problems in the time/space matrix.
2. Identify candidate solutions for the relevant situations.
3. Compare benefits with costs, including training and IS support.
4. Assess security, provider dependence, vendor stability, and switching risks.
5. Involve prospective users to discover adoption and training issues.
6. Shortlist candidates and examine vendor demonstrations against the requirements.

> [!QUIZ]
> 2B Q2. Branches in different time zones need to maintain a shared procedure. Would requiring a daily live meeting solve the main problem? Propose a better tool mix.

> [!ANSWER]
> A mandatory live meeting creates a timing problem and may leave no reusable record. An asynchronous, versioned procedure with discussion and an owner is a better foundation; occasional live meetings can resolve disagreement. Choose tools for the work and preserve decisions after discussion.

## 3. Make knowledge available after the conversation ends

Read §2.8. The café's experienced employee can recognize an equipment problem from a sound. Another employee has written a troubleshooting checklist. Both involve knowledge, but they need different management approaches.

| Distinction | Meaning | How to support it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tacit knowledge | Experience or expertise not formally documented | Expert directories, mentoring, joint work |
| Explicit knowledge | Knowledge that has been documented | Searchable, maintained procedures and reports |
| Structured / semi-structured / unstructured content | How content is organized | Suitable classification, metadata, and retrieval |

> [!CAUTION]
> **Tacit/explicit and structured/unstructured are different distinctions.** A recorded expert interview is explicit because it has been documented, even if its video content is unstructured. Not all expertise can be fully captured in a checklist.

A useful learning flow is **create/acquire → store and organize → share → apply → review**. This paraphrases the activities of knowledge management; do not mistake it for a separate figure reproduced from this chapter. A repository creates value when someone can find, trust, and apply its contents.

## 4. Separate the three knowledge-system families

Inspect **Figure 2.9** (18th book p.58 / PDF 87), then Figures **2.10–2.11** (pp.59–60 / PDF 88–89).

| Family | Main job | Examples in this chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise-wide KMS | Collect, organize, share, and use knowledge across the firm | ECM, collaboration/social tools, LMS |
| Knowledge Work Systems · KWS | Support specialists creating new knowledge | CAD and specialized modeling/visualization workstations |
| Intelligent techniques | Discover patterns, capture expertise, or apply knowledge | Data mining, machine learning, expert systems and other AI methods |

An **enterprise content management system (ECM)** organizes documents and other content in a usable repository. A **taxonomy** defines categories; **tags** attach classification to items. The point is retrieval and interpretation. A folder containing thousands of unnamed files is not a strong knowledge system.

Expert directories help locate the person who knows, while an **LMS** manages learning delivery, tracking, and assessment. They solve different parts of the knowledge problem. An LMS completion record does not necessarily prove that a worker can apply the skill.

A **KWS** helps engineers, scientists, designers, and similar workers produce new knowledge. It may need powerful computation, graphics, simulation, external knowledge access, and a usable interface. General office tools help these workers too, but their presence alone does not make a system a specialized KWS.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Café extension, invented:** an ECM stores approved recipes; an expert directory identifies a roasting specialist; an LMS delivers hygiene training; a specialist uses modeling tools to develop a new production method. Classify each by the knowledge activity it supports, not by whether a computer is involved.

> [!QUIZ]
> 2B Q3. An AI assistant retrieves an old procedure and confidently presents it as current. Which knowledge-management problems remain even if its answer is fluent?

> [!ANSWER]
> Version control, ownership, review dates, access rules, source quality, and validation still matter. Retrieval and generated wording do not establish that a procedure is current or applicable. The knowledge lifecycle needs review and correction, not just storage and search.

## 5. Who takes responsibility for the system?

Read §2.9. **Programmers** implement software. **Systems analysts** translate business requirements into information and system requirements and connect business users with technical teams. **IS managers** coordinate teams and operations. **End users** contribute the business knowledge necessary to design useful systems.

| Leadership role | Main responsibility in the textbook |
| --- | --- |
| CIO · Chief Information Officer | Overall IS/IT direction and alignment with business strategy |
| CSO / CISO | Information security policy and protection |
| CPO · Chief Privacy Officer | Personal-data privacy obligations |
| CKO · Chief Knowledge Officer | Organizational knowledge-management program |
| CDO · Chief Data Officer | Governance and effective use of enterprise data |
| CAIO · Chief AI Officer | AI strategy, acquisition, implementation, and monitoring; emphasized in the 18th edition |

**IT governance** specifies decision rights, policies, and accountability: who chooses investments, who sets data rules, who approves access, and how results are reviewed. It also addresses centralization versus local discretion. A job-title list does not answer these governance questions by itself.

**Transition to Chapter 3:** changing information access changes authority, incentives, and power. This is why a technically workable system can encounter resistance.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 2B — Why is a wiki not a complete knowledge-management solution?

> [!FLASHCARD]

> A: KWS supports specialists creating new knowledge; enterprise-wide KMS makes knowledge and content available across the firm.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 2B — What does the time/space matrix prevent?
> A: Choosing tools by popularity without checking whether participants must work at the same time or in the same place.

> [!QUIZ]
> 2B Q4. Design a learning loop for branches that repeat the same equipment failure. Include people, tools, governance, and an outcome measure.

> [!ANSWER]
> Staff record the failure and consult an expert; the expert and branch team validate a procedure; a named owner publishes and versions it in the ECM; the LMS or a short demonstration supports training; later incidents trigger review. Governance defines approval and access. Measure repeat failures and resolution time, not only document views.
