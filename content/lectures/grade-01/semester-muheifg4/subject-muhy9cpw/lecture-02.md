## Chapter Overview

Organizations are collections of business processes that move materials, information, and knowledge. Information systems improve these processes by automating routine steps, integrating work across functions, supporting decisions, and enabling new ways of operating. Different management levels require different types of systems, but those systems must exchange reliable information rather than operate as isolated silos.

Enterprise applications provide cross-functional integration. Collaboration systems help people work together across time and location. Knowledge-management systems capture, store, distribute, and apply organizational knowledge. The information systems department provides the governance, expertise, and infrastructure needed to coordinate these capabilities.

## 1. Opening Case: Sharp Corporation

Sharp faced hierarchical, top-down processes, weak knowledge sharing, and growing competitive pressure. It adopted enterprise social networking to redesign collaboration and knowledge dissemination. The case demonstrates that collaboration technology can support innovation and growth only when business processes and organizational culture also change.

## 2. Business Processes and Information Systems

### Business Processes

A business process is a logically related set of tasks that defines how a specific business result is achieved. Processes involve flows of material, information, and knowledge. They may exist within one function or cross several functions.

Examples include:

- Manufacturing and production: assembling and checking products
- Sales and marketing: identifying customers and selling products
- Finance and accounting: creating financial statements and paying creditors
- Human resources: hiring employees and evaluating performance

Processes can be organizational assets when they are efficient and responsive. They can become liabilities when they preserve outdated practices or obstruct coordination.

### Order Fulfillment

The order-fulfillment process crosses sales, accounting, and manufacturing. It illustrates why important processes require coordination across departmental boundaries rather than optimization of only one function.

### How IT Improves Processes

Information technology can:

- Automate manual steps
- Increase the efficiency of existing work
- Change the flow of information
- Replace sequential steps with parallel work
- Remove delays in decision-making
- Enable entirely new business processes and business models

## 3. Systems for Different Management Groups

| System | Primary users | Decision or work supported | Typical output or use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transaction Processing System (TPS) | Operational managers and staff | Routine daily transactions | Sales orders, payroll, shipping, receipts |
| Management Information System (MIS) | Middle management | Structured monitoring and routine decisions | Periodic summaries and exception reports based largely on TPS data |
| Decision Support System (DSS) | Middle management and analysts | Nonroutine and semi-structured decisions | Models, simulations, external data, and what-if analysis |
| Executive Support System (ESS) | Senior management | Strategic, unstructured decisions | Dashboards and integrated internal and external information |

### Transaction Processing Systems

TPS record the recurring transactions needed to run the business. They are essential because other systems depend on the accuracy and completeness of transaction data. The payroll example shows how employee time and payment data produce paychecks, reports, and accounting entries.

### Management Information Systems

MIS summarize operational data to show current performance. They answer routine questions through predefined reporting procedures and typically offer limited analytical flexibility.

### Decision Support Systems

DSS support nonroutine decisions by combining data with analytical models. A voyage-estimating system, for example, can help managers select vessels, loading patterns, destinations, and travel speeds while balancing cost, schedules, and profit.

### Executive Support Systems

ESS present high-level internal and external information in a simple form. They help senior leaders exercise judgment about strategic issues. ESS rely on trustworthy information from MIS and DSS, so separation among these systems can create organizational dysfunction.

## 4. Enterprise Applications

Enterprise applications integrate processes and information across functions, management levels, and sometimes organizational boundaries.

### Enterprise Systems and ERP

Enterprise systems, often called enterprise resource planning systems, collect data from multiple functions and store it in a common repository. They can:

- Accelerate communication across the firm
- Improve the accuracy of order fulfillment
- Increase flexibility in responding to customers
- Provide managers with an integrated view of operations
- Standardize cross-functional processes

### Supply Chain Management Systems

SCM systems coordinate suppliers, purchasing firms, distributors, and logistics providers. They share information about orders, production, inventory, and delivery. The goal is to move the correct quantity of product from source to consumption quickly and at low cost.

### Customer Relationship Management Systems

CRM systems coordinate customer-facing processes in sales, marketing, and service. Their goals include improving satisfaction, increasing retention, and optimizing revenue by providing a consistent view of the customer relationship.

### Knowledge Management Systems

KMS capture and apply expertise about how the organization creates, produces, and delivers value. They make useful knowledge available where it is needed and connect the firm to external knowledge sources.

### Enterprise Application Architecture

The architecture connects enterprise systems, SCM, CRM, and KMS across core functions such as sales, manufacturing, finance, and human resources. It also links the firm to suppliers, business partners, customers, and distributors.

## 5. Intranets, Extranets, and Digital Business

- **Intranet:** an internal network using Internet standards and restricted to employees.
- **Extranet:** a controlled network area accessible to authorized external partners such as suppliers or vendors.
- **E-business:** use of digital technology and the Internet to run major business processes.
- **E-commerce:** the portion of e-business concerned with buying and selling through the Internet.
- **E-government:** use of Internet technology to provide public information and services.

## 6. Collaboration and Social Business

### Collaboration

Collaboration can be short-term or long-term, informal or formal, and may involve permanent teams or temporary groups. Its importance has increased because work is more knowledge intensive, organizations are more distributed, and many problems require interaction across specialties.

### Social Business

Social business uses internal and external social-networking platforms to engage employees, customers, and suppliers. It aims to deepen interaction, speed information sharing, strengthen relationships, and increase transparency.

### Business Benefits

Effective collaboration can improve:

- Productivity through shared knowledge and faster problem solving
- Quality through faster identification and resolution of issues
- Innovation through a larger pool of ideas
- Customer service through quicker handling of requests and complaints
- Financial performance through improvements in the preceding areas

### Organizational Requirements

Technology is only one requirement for collaboration. Organizations also need:

- A collaborative culture
- Supportive leadership
- Team-based processes
- Appropriate incentives
- Decentralized participation where suitable
- Clear rules for sharing, privacy, and accountability

Command-and-control cultures often limit collaboration because decisions and information remain concentrated at the top.

### Collaboration Technologies

Tools include:

- Email and instant messaging
- Wikis
- Virtual worlds and immersive environments
- Video and telepresence systems
- Cloud document and workspace services
- Enterprise social-networking software with profiles, feeds, groups, shared content, tagging, search, permissions, and privacy controls

### Selecting Collaboration Tools

Managers should identify the organization's collaboration problems, evaluate available solutions, compare costs and benefits, consider security and integration, and examine how users will adopt the tools.

The time-space matrix classifies collaboration by whether participants work:

- At the same time and in the same place
- At the same time but in different places
- At different times in the same place
- At different times and in different places

## 7. Knowledge Management in Business

Knowledge and other intangible assets contribute substantially to organizational value. Knowledge-management systems are designed to make this resource reusable rather than leaving it fragmented across documents, systems, and individual employees.

### Data, Information, Knowledge, and Wisdom

- **Data:** raw observations or facts.
- **Information:** data organized into a meaningful form.
- **Knowledge:** information interpreted through experience, rules, patterns, and context.
- **Wisdom:** informed judgment about when, where, and how to apply knowledge.

### Dimensions of Knowledge

- Knowledge is an intangible firm asset.
- Knowledge may be explicit and documented or tacit and embedded in individual experience.
- It may take the form of know-how, craft, skill, procedures, causal understanding, or contextual judgment.
- Knowledge is often socially situated, organization-specific, and difficult to transfer.
- Sharing knowledge can create network effects because its value grows as more people use and improve it.

### Organizational Learning

Organizations learn by collecting data, measuring results, experimenting, receiving feedback, and changing behavior. Learning may lead to new processes or different patterns of managerial decision-making.

## 8. The Knowledge-Management Value Chain

Knowledge management consists of processes for creating, storing, transferring, and applying knowledge.

### 8.1 Knowledge Acquisition

- Document explicit and tacit knowledge
- Store reports, presentations, best practices, and communications
- Build expert networks
- Create new knowledge
- Capture data from transaction systems and external sources

### 8.2 Knowledge Storage

- Use databases and document-management systems
- Apply classification schemes and repositories
- Establish management support for retention and organization

### 8.3 Knowledge Dissemination

- Use portals, wikis, email, messaging, search, and collaboration tools
- Combine technical distribution with training, informal networks, and shared experience
- Manage information overload so attention remains focused on useful material

### 8.4 Knowledge Application

- Embed knowledge in decisions, processes, products, and services
- Connect new knowledge to existing transaction and enterprise systems
- Create feedback that generates further knowledge and improvement

## 9. Organizational and Management Capital for Knowledge

Knowledge systems need supporting roles and communities. Examples include chief knowledge officers, knowledge managers, and communities of practice. Communities of practice are informal professional networks that share expertise, educate members, and help develop common methods.

## 10. Major Types of Knowledge-Management Systems

### Enterprise-Wide Knowledge-Management Systems

These systems collect, store, distribute, and apply structured, semistructured, and tacit knowledge throughout the firm.

### Knowledge Work Systems

KWS support specialists who create new knowledge, including researchers, designers, architects, scientists, and engineers. They need substantial computing power, analytical and graphics tools, communication and document-management capabilities, links to external databases, and interfaces optimized for specialized tasks.

Examples include:

- Computer-aided design
- 3D printing
- Virtual reality
- Augmented reality
- Specialized financial, engineering, and scientific workstations

### Intelligent Techniques

Intelligent techniques extend the organizational knowledge base. Examples include expert systems, case-based reasoning, fuzzy logic, neural networks, data mining, and genetic algorithms.

## 11. Enterprise Content and Learning Systems

### Enterprise Content Management

Content-management systems capture, classify, store, retrieve, distribute, and preserve documents and semistructured information. They require metadata, taxonomies, access controls, and rules that distinguish authoritative content from obsolete or duplicate material.

### Locating Expertise

Directories, search tools, tagging, and social networks help employees find people with relevant knowledge inside or outside the organization.

### Learning Management Systems

LMS support the administration, delivery, tracking, and assessment of employee education. They can provide web-based classes, forums, course assignment, content delivery, and measurement of learning effectiveness.

## 12. The Information Systems Function

The information systems department is the formal organizational unit responsible for technology services. It may include leadership roles such as:

- Chief Information Officer
- Chief Security or Information Security Officer
- Chief Knowledge Officer
- Chief Privacy Officer
- Chief Data Officer

End users increasingly participate in system design and development. IT governance defines decision rights, accountability, standards, priorities, and policies for technology use.

## 14. Career Relevance

The chapter connects business systems knowledge to roles such as sales support, business analysis, operations, enterprise applications, and information systems management. Useful skills include understanding cross-functional processes, interpreting system-generated information, coordinating with users and technical teams, and evaluating whether a proposed system fits organizational goals.

## Core Takeaway

Business performance depends on connected processes and connected information. Transaction, management, decision, executive, enterprise, collaboration, and knowledge systems create the most value when they operate as an integrated organizational system supported by appropriate culture, governance, and management.
