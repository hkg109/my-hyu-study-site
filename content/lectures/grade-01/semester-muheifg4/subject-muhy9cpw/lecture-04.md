# Chapter 3: Information Systems, Organizations, and Strategy

## Study Guide

*pp. 1-3*

This chapter explains the two-way relationship between organizations and information technology, then connects information systems to industry competition and firm strategy.

| Guiding question | Core concept |
|---|---|
| How should we understand an organization? | Technical and behavioral views |
| How does IT change organizations? | Transaction costs, agency costs, flattening |
| How can industry competition be analyzed? | Porter's five competitive forces |
| Where can a firm find strategic opportunities? | Value chain and value web |
| Why can networks become more valuable as they grow? | Synergies, core competencies, network economics, ecosystems |

> [!EXAMPLE]
> PepsiCo analyzed more than 5,000 crunch sounds with AI and machine learning to create Doritos Silent, a software tool for gamers. The case shows how IT can reshape customer experience and market strategy rather than merely automate internal work.

## The Two-Way Relationship Between Organizations and IT

*pp. 4-5*

Information technology and organizations influence each other. Structure, business processes, politics, culture, the environment, and management decisions shape system design; new systems then reshape work, authority, and decision-making.

| Organization shapes IT | IT shapes the organization |
|---|---|
| Work rules define requirements | Information access and decision speed change |
| Culture and politics affect adoption | Jobs, authority, and hierarchy may be redesigned |
| Managers determine investment and scope | New products, services, and strategies become possible |

> [!EXAM]
> ==Organizations and information technology are interdependent.== The same technology can produce very different results depending on organizational structure, culture, politics, and managerial choices.

## Two Views of Organizations

*pp. 6-8*

| View | Definition | View of change |
|---|---|---|
| Technical | A formal structure that transforms capital and labor from the environment into products and services | Relatively malleable, like rearranging machine parts |
| Behavioral | A collection of rights, duties, privileges, and relationships balanced through conflict and conflict resolution | Slow and difficult because people, values, routines, and obligations are involved |

The views are complementary: the technical model clarifies inputs and outputs, while the behavioral model explains resistance and the difficulty of organizational change.

## Common Features of Organizations

*pp. 9-18*

### Routines and Business Processes

Routines, or standard operating procedures, are precise rules and practices for expected situations.

`individual routines -> business processes -> the firm`

High performance from a new system usually requires changes to the routines and processes around it.

### Politics and Culture

| Feature | Meaning | Effect on systems |
|---|---|---|
| Organizational politics | Competition and conflict among different viewpoints and interests | Resistance arises when information and authority are redistributed |
| Organizational culture | Shared assumptions about what, how, where, and for whom the organization produces | Unifies members but can restrain changes that challenge core assumptions |

### Environments and Disruptive Technology

Organizations depend on and influence their social and physical environments. Because environments often change faster than organizations, information systems act as a lens for environmental scanning.

Disruptive technologies can transform products, industries, and markets. First movers invent them, but fast followers with resources and execution capacity may capture more value.

### Organizational Structures

| Structure | Typical organization | Main characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Entrepreneurial | Small startup | Simple structure and founder control |
| Machine bureaucracy | Midsize manufacturer | Standard procedures and centralized control |
| Divisionalized bureaucracy | Fortune 500 firm | Product- or region-based divisions |
| Professional bureaucracy | Hospital, school, law firm | Expertise and professional qualification |
| Adhocracy | Consulting or project organization | Flexible teams of specialists |

Goals, constituencies, leadership styles, task types, and environments also vary, so systems must fit the organization rather than assume a universal design.

## Organizational Impact of Information Systems

*pp. 19-27*

### Economic Impact

| Theory | Cost being explained | Effect of IT |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction cost theory | Cost of finding suppliers, contracting, and monitoring market exchanges | Lowers market costs and makes outsourcing or smaller firms practical |
| Agency theory | Cost of managing and supervising self-interested agents | Improves visibility and span of control, limiting growth in managerial layers |

### Flattening Organizations

Information systems give lower-level employees decision information and allow managers to supervise more people. In postindustrial organizations, authority can shift from formal position toward knowledge and competence.

### Resistance to Change

| Element that must change | What to examine |
|---|---|
| Technology | Nature and difficulty of the innovation |
| Tasks | Changes to workflows and roles |
| Structure | Changes to authority and reporting relationships |
| People | Skills, attitudes, incentives, and acceptance |

> [!CAUTION]
> Information systems alter access to a critical resource: information. Implementation is therefore both a technical project and an organizational change project involving authority, culture, jobs, and routines.

### Design Checklist

- Environment and regulation
- Hierarchy, specialization, routines, and processes
- Culture and political interests
- Leadership style and affected constituencies
- User attitudes, tasks, decisions, and processes to be supported

## Porter's Five Competitive Forces

*pp. 28-31*

| Force | Key question | Pressure increases when... |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional competitors | How intense is rivalry in the current market? | Price rivalry is high and differentiation is low |
| New entrants | Can new firms enter easily? | Entry barriers are low and technology is standardized |
| Substitutes | Can another offering satisfy the same need? | Switching is easy and alternatives offer better value |
| Customers | Can buyers dictate price and terms? | Buyers have many choices and low switching costs |
| Suppliers | Can suppliers dictate price and terms? | Alternatives are scarce and suppliers control critical inputs |

## IS Strategies for Competitive Forces

*pp. 32-36*

| Strategy | Role of information systems | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Low-cost leadership | Optimize inventory, supply, and operations | Walmart continuous replenishment |
| Product differentiation | Enable new features, experiences, and mass customization | Google, Nike, Apple |
| Focus on a market niche | Analyze segments and specialize for a target group | Hilton OnQ |
| Customer and supplier intimacy | Use CRM and SCM to strengthen loyalty and ties | Amazon, Toyota, Starbucks |

The Internet increases transparency and rivalry but also creates opportunities to build brands and loyal communities. IoT-based smart products combine physical goods with information-intensive services, supporting differentiation and switching costs.

## The Value Chain and Value Web

*pp. 37-40*

| Activity type | Representative activities | IS opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, sales and marketing, service | Automation, inventory optimization, customer experience |
| Support | Firm infrastructure, HR, technology development, procurement | Enterprise data, talent systems, analytics, e-procurement |

Porter's model identifies **which external forces** matter; the value-chain model shows **which internal activities** can be improved. A value web synchronizes the value chains of suppliers, distributors, customers, and other independent firms in a flexible, demand-driven network.

## Synergies, Core Competencies, and Network Strategies

*pp. 41-47*

- **Synergy:** business units share outputs, markets, or expertise to reduce cost and increase profit.
- **Core competency:** a world-class activity that competitors struggle to copy, strengthened by sharing knowledge across units.

| Network strategy | Principle | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Network economics | Marginal cost of another participant is low while network value grows | Community and installed-base effects |
| Virtual company | Partners produce and distribute without traditional boundaries | Li & Fung supplier network |
| Business ecosystem | Firms from multiple industries create value around a platform | Keystone and niche firms are interdependent |

## AI in Competitive Strategy

*p. 48*

AI can improve demand forecasting, production, personalized marketing, customer service, and procurement across the value chain. It can also detect changes in entrants, substitutes, customer churn, and supplier risk.

> [!ADVANCED]
> AI alone is rarely a sustainable advantage because competitors can buy similar models and services. It becomes harder to imitate when combined with proprietary data, distinctive processes, talent, and organizational learning.

## Challenges of Strategic Information Systems

*p. 49*

- Competitors can imitate or retaliate against strategic systems.
- A competitive advantage can become an industry requirement for survival.
- Investment should follow analysis of industry structure and firm and industry value chains.
- Technology must remain aligned with strategy, organization, and processes.

## Career Connection and Final Review

*p. 50*

Business development, product, consulting, and analytics roles require the ability to connect industry structure and customer needs to revenue, cost, relationships, and differentiation.

### One-Minute Review

- The theory explaining the cost of using markets is {{transaction cost theory}}.
- The theory explaining internal management and supervision cost is {{agency theory}}.
- The five forces include competitors, entrants, {{substitutes}}, customers, and suppliers.
- The model for internal value-adding activities is the {{value chain}}.
- A synchronized network of independent firms' value chains is a {{value web}}.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: How do Porter's competitive-forces model and the value-chain model differ?
> A: Porter's model analyzes external industry pressures; the value-chain model identifies specific internal activities where a firm can lower cost or differentiate.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Why must technology, tasks, structure, and people change together?
> A: They are interdependent. Changing only the technology can conflict with routines, authority, culture, and incentives, causing resistance and weak performance.

> [!QUIZ]
> A platform can add users at almost zero marginal cost, and every participant gains more value as membership grows. Which concept explains this?

> [!ANSWER]
> Network economics, or the network effect: a larger installed base creates more connections, content, and transaction opportunities, increasing value for the whole network.
