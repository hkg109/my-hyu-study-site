## 1. Organizations and systems shape each other

Our invented café chain wants every branch to use a demand forecast. The software works, but branch managers ignore it because they lose autonomy over ordering. This is not just a training problem: the system changes who controls a decision.

> [!DEFINITION]
> The **technical view** treats an organization as a formal structure that transforms inputs into outputs. The **behavioral view** emphasizes relationships, rights, obligations, privileges, values, and the compromises through which people work together.

The technical view helps describe resources and production. The behavioral view helps explain why a seemingly efficient redesign meets resistance. Use both: a process diagram can show a new approval route, while a behavioral explanation shows who loses influence under it.

**Organization → system design → changed information access and work → organizational response → revised design.** This is a reciprocal relationship. Avoid claiming that technology alone dictates the final organization.

### Features to notice before recommending a system

| Feature | What to examine | Why it affects an IS project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routines and processes | Established rules for recurring situations | A new workflow may conflict with habitual work |
| Politics | Groups with different interests and resources | More transparent information can redistribute power |
| Culture | Shared assumptions about what matters | A system may challenge accepted ways of working |
| Environment | Customers, suppliers, regulation, competitors | External change creates pressure and uncertainty |
| Structure | Hierarchy, specialization, coordination | Different organizations need different decision support |
| Goals and leadership | Priorities, incentives, styles of control | The same tool may serve different purposes |

The textbook's structure discussion includes entrepreneurial structures, machine bureaucracies, divisionalized bureaucracies, professional bureaucracies, and adhocracies. Compare a small owner-led café with a large standardized chain or a professional organization. Do not assume one reporting structure fits all of them.

## 2. Follow the cost that technology changes

Read §3.3 carefully. Two theories are easy to mix up because both involve coordination.

| Theory | Main question | Costs concerned | Possible IS effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transaction cost theory | Should we obtain work through the market or inside the firm? | Finding partners, negotiating, communicating, monitoring contracts | Lower external coordination costs can make outsourcing more attractive |
| Agency theory | How can a principal ensure agents act appropriately? | Monitoring, coordination, and managing diverging interests | Better information can reduce internal supervision costs |

In the café, comparing suppliers and monitoring delivery agreements concerns **transaction costs**. Giving regional managers reliable branch records to supervise operations concerns **agency costs**. Buying the coffee itself is a production/input expense, not the entire transaction-cost concept.

IT can support flatter hierarchies by giving frontline workers information and letting managers oversee more people. But this is a possible organizational effect, not a guarantee. Firms must still choose decision rights, controls, and incentives. Lower costs do not prove that every business will shrink or decentralize.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q1. Classify the main cost reduced: (a) a portal compares supplier offers and tracks contract fulfillment; (b) a dashboard lets one manager supervise more branches. Explain why the distinction matters.

> [!ANSWER]
> (a) Primarily **transaction costs**, associated with using and coordinating through the market. (b) Primarily **agency costs**, associated with internal monitoring and coordination. The distinction identifies the mechanism; calling both “lower operating cost” misses the causal explanation.

### Resistance is evidence to investigate

A new system may change tasks, structure, technology, and people simultaneously. If managers lose discretion, staff gain extra recording work, or experienced employees lose status, resistance may be understandable. Ask what work and incentives must change, and involve affected groups. Do not explain every failure as employees being irrational or unwilling to learn.

## 3. Diagnose pressure with Porter's five forces

Read §3.4 and inspect its competitive-forces diagram. The model asks **what affects industry competition and the firm's position**, beyond its direct rivals.

| Force | Diagnostic question | Café application, invented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing competitors | How intense is rivalry among established firms? | Nearby cafés compete on price, speed, and taste |
| New entrants | How easy is it to enter? | New sellers can reach customers through delivery platforms |
| Substitutes | What different offering meets the same need? | Packed lunches or convenience-store meals replace café lunch |
| Customers | Can buyers compare, negotiate, or switch easily? | Price comparison and low switching effort increase pressure |
| Suppliers | How dependent is the firm on particular sources? | A unique ingredient with few suppliers limits alternatives |

A **substitute** is not simply another brand of the same product. It satisfies a similar need differently. **Switching costs** are the effort, expense, or loss associated with moving to another provider; they are not identical to a product's price.

### Match a response to the pressure

| IS-supported response | How it works | What to explain in a case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-cost leadership | Improve operating efficiency | Which activity becomes cheaper without undermining required quality? |
| Product differentiation | Offer valued features or experience | Why would customers prefer this offering? |
| Focus on a market niche | Use information to serve a specific segment | Which segment has a distinct need? |
| Customer/supplier intimacy | Strengthen relationships and coordination | What useful integration makes relationships harder to replace? |

The Internet can lower entry and search costs, increase price transparency, and expand rivalry. It can also create new markets and services. State **which force changes, through which mechanism, for which actor**; “the Internet increases competition” alone is incomplete.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q2. The café launches a service tailored to students with a short lunch break. Customers can rely on an exact pickup window. Name a strategy and explain why an app alone is not sufficient.

> [!ANSWER]
> It can combine **niche focus** with **differentiation**. The segment values time reliability. The advantage requires accurate capacity information, preparation routines, and dependable handoff; a copied app interface does not deliver those capabilities by itself.

## 4. Locate the intervention with the value chain

Five forces identifies pressures. The **value chain** identifies activities where changes may improve cost or customer value. Use the models in sequence instead of treating them as competing answers.

Inspect **Figure 3.9** (book p.90 / PDF 119). Primary activities directly contribute to producing and delivering the offering; support activities enable that work.

| Primary activity | Café application | Possible system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inbound logistics | Receive and store ingredients | Inventory and receiving records |
| Operations | Prepare food | Production scheduling and quality tracking |
| Outbound logistics | Dispatch or hand over orders | Pickup/delivery scheduling |
| Sales and marketing | Reach customers and accept orders | Ordering and campaign systems |
| Service | Handle questions and complaints | Customer service records |

**Support activities:** administration/management, human resources, procurement, and technology development. Procurement supports purchasing decisions; inbound logistics handles receiving and storing what arrives. They are related but not identical.

**Benchmarking** compares performance against standards or other organizations. **Best practices** are effective methods identified from experience. Adopting a practice still requires checking whether it fits this firm's context.

A **value web**, shown in **Figure 3.10** (book p.92 / PDF 121), connects partners' value chains so they can coordinate dynamically. It is a network of cooperating firms and activities, not merely a longer internal chain.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q3. Which model should you start with for (a) supplier bargaining pressure, (b) delays in warehouse receiving, and (c) coordinating several partners' value-adding activities?

> [!ANSWER]
> (a) **Five forces** diagnoses external competitive pressure. (b) The **value chain** locates an internal activity and possible improvement. (c) A **value web** examines connected partner activities. A complete recommendation may use all three in sequence.

## 5. Go beyond a single business unit

Read §3.6 and ask where the extra value comes from.

| Concept | Mechanism | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synergy | Units share inputs, outputs, markets, or expertise | Value comes from combining operations |
| Core competency | A deeply developed activity in which the firm excels | Value comes from accumulated expertise, supported by knowledge sharing |
| Network economics | Participation makes a network more valuable | Value comes from useful connections, not just cheaper production |
| Virtual company | Networks coordinate capabilities across firm boundaries | The firm can use assets without owning them all |
| Business ecosystem | Multiple firms or industries jointly deliver value around platforms | Includes complementary roles and often a keystone platform firm |

A growing messaging network gives users more people to reach. That is a network effect. Buying ingredients in bulk at a discount is an economy of scale, not automatically a network effect. More users do not guarantee quality: congestion, poor matching, or harmful interactions can offset benefits.

Link this section back to Chapter 2B: knowledge sharing supports core competencies; coordinating partners depends on reliable information and governance.

## 6. AI changes possibilities, not the need for strategy

Read Tables **3.6–3.7**, book pp.95–96 / PDF 124–125. AI can support forecasting, quality inspection, recommendations, service, logistics, and recruitment. For each application, identify the value-chain activity and the intended business outcome.

AI can also alter competitive forces in different directions. Data, expertise, and computing requirements may raise entry barriers, while available platforms may lower some barriers. Better customer experiences may encourage loyalty, while new comparison tools may strengthen buyers. Explain the particular mechanism rather than claiming one universal effect.

> [!ADVANCED]
> To evaluate an AI proposal, connect **task → data → prediction or generated output → human/organizational action → result**. Then ask what a rival can copy and what depends on distinctive data, knowledge, or execution. “Uses AI” is not a sufficient explanation of advantage.

### Sustainability and alignment

Read §3.8. A strategic system can be expensive, hard to implement, and easy to imitate. **Alignment** means connecting IT choices to business goals and the organizational changes needed to achieve them. Sustainable advantage requires ongoing adaptation, not a one-time purchase.

A useful answer checks three levels: **industry pressure**, **value-adding activities**, and **organizational readiness**. It also names a metric and a risk. For the café's pickup promise, track late orders and customer retention while checking whether kitchen overload makes the promise unrealistic.

- **AI and fashion**, pp.101–102: choose one application, place it in the value chain, and explain both the value and the requirements for delivering it.

## 8. Retrieval and a strategic recommendation

- External search and contracting belong to {{transaction costs}}.
- Internal monitoring of agents relates to {{agency costs}}.
- Industry pressure is analyzed with {{five forces}}; activities with the {{value chain}}.
- Coordinated partner value chains form a {{value web}}.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Why do technical and behavioral views both matter?
> A: One explains production and formal structure; the other explains interests, rights, and relationships that affect implementation.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Network effects versus economies of scale?
> A: Network effects increase value through participation and connections; economies of scale reduce average cost through greater production or purchasing scale.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Why might an IS advantage disappear?
> A: Rivals can imitate it, customer needs can change, implementation may fail, or the business model may not cover its costs.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q4. Recommend a strategy for the café chain in six linked steps: force, response, activity, system, organizational condition, and durability risk.

> [!ANSWER]
> Example: Customers can switch easily. Differentiate through dependable short-window pickup. Improve operations and outbound handoff. Integrate ordering, capacity, and preparation data. Give staff clear capacity limits and authority to stop accepting impossible slots. Rivals can copy the interface, so the firm must sustain reliable execution and improve the service rather than rely on the app alone.
