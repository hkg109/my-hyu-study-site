### Slides 1–18 · understand the organization before prescribing technology

#### Technical versus behavioral view

| View | Organization as | Strength | Easy to miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Technical | Formal structure transforming capital/labor into outputs | Input/output and efficiency | Conflict, rights, culture, resistance |
| Behavioral | Social balance of rights, duties, relationships, hierarchy, conflict | Real change and politics | Quantifiable production relation |

#### Six-part organization diagnosis

- **Structure:** entrepreneurial, machine, divisional, professional, adhocracy.
- **Routine/process:** individual SOPs combine into processes; processes combine into the firm.
- **Politics:** interests clash when information, budget, and authority move.
- **Culture:** taken-for-granted beliefs about what, how, where, and for whom work is done.
- **Environment:** the firm senses and influences customer, regulation, technology, and economy.
- **Management choice:** standardization, authority, reward, and pace change the outcome of the same tool.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Doritos Silent matters as `gamer distraction → 5,000+ crunch samples → AI/ML software → segment experience → brand/sales`, not as “PepsiCo used AI.”

#### Explanation

The video cases point to GE's industrial Internet and the NBA's Akamai streaming delivery. Nine objectives move from technical/behavioral views and organizational features through IS impacts, competitive forces, value chain, synergies/core competencies/network economics, AI strategy, strategic-system challenges, and careers. The opening PepsiCo case begins with a narrow customer problem: gamers were distracted by other people's Doritos crunching, while consumers liked the product's crunch. Developers analyzed more than 5,000 crunch sounds with AI/ML to create Doritos Silent software, aiming to preserve appeal in the gaming segment and support sales. The strategic value comes from linking a technology to a segment and product experience, not from AI alone.

Figures 3.1–3.3 establish two complementary views. Technically, an organization is a formal social structure transforming environmental capital and labor into products and services, with outputs and feedback. Behaviorally, it is a historically balanced collection of rights, privileges, duties, relationships, hierarchy, contracts, rules, and conflict resolution. IT and the organization influence each other through structure, process, politics, culture, environment, and managerial choices; prediction is difficult because people and institutions make the organization less mechanically adjustable.

Organizations share hierarchy, authority, accountability, efficiency, routines, processes, politics, culture, environment, and structure. **Routines/SOPs** handle expected situations; collections of routines become processes; collections of processes form the firm. Figure 3.4 therefore implies that a new system must change routines and processes to produce higher performance. Politics arises from divergent interests and can resist or improve a change. Culture defines taken-for-granted answers about what the organization makes, how, where, and for whom; it unifies but can restrain technological change.

Organizations depend on and influence their environments. IS can scan the environment like a lens, but a badly designed lens can hide threats. Disruptive technologies create sweeping market change through substitute performance; first movers invent, while fast followers may have more resources to commercialize. Mintzberg's five structures are entrepreneurial, machine bureaucracy, divisionalized bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, and adhocracy; the system often reflects the structure. Goals, constituencies, leadership style, routine/nonroutine tasks, and environment also differ, so prisons, businesses, colleges, hospitals, and consultancies should not receive identical designs.

### Slides 19–27 · trace costs, authority, and resistance

#### Two economic theories

| Theory | Cost reduced | IT contribution | Possible organization change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transaction cost | Search, contract, purchase, market monitoring | Cheaper partner discovery and coordination | More outsourcing, smaller boundary |
| Agency theory | Internal supervision and coordination | Wider, faster performance information | Wider span, fewer layers |

**Flattening chain:** `wider information access → more lower-level judgment → fewer decision layers → wider managerial span`. Data does not automatically delegate authority.

#### Resistance map

`Innovation ↔ task ↔ structure ↔ people/culture/politics`

> [!CAUTION]
> Do not write only “employees dislike change.” Identify who loses information, expertise, budget, approval authority, or job identity; then distinguish training, participation, incentive, role redesign, and phased transition responses.

#### Explanation

IT changes the relative cost of capital, labor, and information. **Transaction cost theory** explains why firms internalize work when searching, contracting, monitoring, and buying through markets is expensive. Networks lower some market transaction costs, making outsourcing and smaller firm boundaries more attractive. **Agency theory** treats the firm as contracts among self-interested agents who require management and supervision; information can lower agency costs and allow wider span without adding the same number of managers.

Behaviorally, faster information and wider span can flatten the hierarchy, move decisions downward, reduce management layers, and give knowledge/competence more authority than formal position. Figure 3.6 shows faster paths and fewer layers, but empowerment is an organizational choice, not an automatic property of a network.

Systems redistribute access to information and therefore affect politics, tasks, structure, culture, and people. Political and organizational resistance is a leading reason large projects fail. Figure 3.7 says the innovation, structure, culture, and affected tasks must be changed together. The Internet expands access, storage, and distribution and can lower transaction, agency, and manual distribution costs, but still does not erase organizational conditions. Before design, inspect environment; hierarchy, specialization, routines, and processes; culture and politics; organizational type and leadership; affected interest groups and user attitudes; and the tasks/decisions/processes the system will support.

### Slides 28–36 · diagnose the competitive pressure before choosing a response

#### Five-forces question table

| Force | Diagnostic question | What IS may change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry | How intense is price, quality, speed competition? | Cost, quality, experience, response time |
| Entrants | Which barriers, brands, or scale advantages exist? | Data, platform, network, switching cost |
| Substitutes | Can another method satisfy the same need? | Bundling, differentiation, convenience |
| Customers | Can buyers compare, switch, and pressure price? | Personalization, loyalty, CRM, switching cost |
| Suppliers | Are alternatives available and bargaining positions balanced? | Visibility, diversification, joint planning |

**Four responses:** `low-cost leadership / differentiation / niche focus / customer-supplier intimacy`.

> [!EXAM]
> Answer formula: `one force → one response → required information/process → metric → imitation risk`. Listing strategy names is not analysis.

**Smart product/IoT margin:** connected sensors may differentiate, raise switching costs, or deter entry, but standards can enable rivals and connection adds security/data responsibility.

#### Explanation

Porter's model examines traditional competitors, new entrants, substitutes, customers, and suppliers. Existing rivals continuously improve offerings and efficiency; entrants may have new equipment and staff but weak brands or face high barriers; substitutes become attractive when price/value shifts; powerful customers can switch or force price competition; powerful suppliers can raise input costs faster than the firm can raise prices. Figure 3.8 places direct rivals in the same industry while the other four forces apply pressure around it.

Four IS-enabled responses are: **low-cost leadership** (Walmart's continuous replenishment/efficient response); **product differentiation** and mass customization (Google, Nike, Apple); **focus on a market niche** using detailed data (Hilton OnQ); and **customer/supplier intimacy** that creates loyalty and switching cost (Toyota, Amazon, Starbucks). Do not name a strategy without connecting it to the diagnosed force.

The Internet transforms some industries and intensifies rivalry through universal standards and lower entry, while also enabling brand and loyalty building. IoT turns connected sensor-equipped products such as fitness equipment and health trackers into information-rich services. Smart products can differentiate, raise switching costs, discourage entrants, or reduce supplier power, while also provoking stronger rivalry.

### Slides 37–47 · locate value inside and beyond the firm

#### Expanding scope

`Internal value chain → linked partner chains → value web → cross-industry business ecosystem`

| Concept | Core idea | Exam question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value chain | Primary/support activities add value | Which activity should IS improve? |
| Value web | Independent firms synchronize chains | How do partners respond to demand/supply? |
| Synergy | Units combine output, market, expertise | Where is the whole greater than parts? |
| Core competence | Difficult-to-copy world-class knowledge/activity | How is it shared across units? |
| Network economics | More participants can raise value for all | Why do scale and installed base matter? |
| Virtual company | Partners coordinate without owning every asset | How does the network cross boundaries? |
| Ecosystem/platform | Keystone and niche firms are interdependent | Who sets rules, depends, and captures value? |

> [!DEFINITION]
> A **value web** emphasizes synchronized value chains, often within an industry. A **business ecosystem** extends to cross-industry platform participants and their broader interdependence.

#### Explanation

The **value chain** treats a firm as primary and support activities that add value and reveals where IS can improve efficiency or stakeholder intimacy. Benchmarking and best practices help set a reference. Unlike the five-forces diagnosis, the value chain gives a more specific place to intervene. Figure 3.9 includes both the firm's activities and value partners.

A firm's chain links to suppliers, distributors, and customers. A **value web** is a flexible, customer-driven network of independent firms using synchronized IT to coordinate their chains and respond to changing supply and demand. Figure 3.10 emphasizes many-to-many coordination rather than a single linear chain.

**Synergy** occurs when one unit's output becomes another's input or units combine markets and expertise, lowering cost or increasing profit; IS can make separate business units operate as a whole. A **core competency** is a world-class, difficult-to-copy activity grounded in shared knowledge and experience; expert directories and intranets can spread it across units.

Network-based strategies include network economics, virtual companies, and ecosystems. Under **network economics**, adding a participant can cost almost nothing while increasing value for all, unlike ordinary diminishing returns; installed user bases and communities therefore matter. A **virtual company** uses networks to coordinate other firms' production and distribution without owning all factories or locations—Li & Fung is the deck's example. A **business ecosystem** is a loosely coupled, interdependent set of industries and firms. Keystone firms such as major platform owners supply the platform; niche firms depend on it and seek profitable roles. Ecosystems cross industry boundaries, whereas a value web is usually framed around linked value chains in an industry. Figure 3.11 shows competition among ecosystems and dense IT-enabled interactions.

### Slides 48–50 · AI must fit a defensible strategy

#### AI strategy checklist · F-V-D-A

- **Force:** which competitive pressure changes?
- **Value activity:** which primary/support activity changes?
- **Defensibility:** why are the data, process, relationship, or learning effect hard to copy?
- **Alignment:** does it fit structure, culture, skill, accountability, and metric?

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Why may strategic-system advantage fail to last?
> A: Rivals imitate or retaliate, technology becomes an industry requirement, and weak complementary assets or alignment erase differentiation.

> [!QUIZ]
> A delivery platform adds AI recommendations. What else is required before this becomes a strategy explanation?

> [!ANSWER]

#### Explanation

The deck asks students to apply AI to both the value chain and Porter's forces: identify the activity or force, show the changed cost/experience/information/relationship, and test whether the result is hard to copy. Strategic systems attract retaliation and imitation, so an advantage may become merely a survival requirement. Alignment requires strategic analysis of industry structure, the firm's and industry's value chains, capabilities, and management choices—not a technology shopping list.

## 1. Organizations and systems shape each other

Our invented café chain wants every branch to use a demand forecast. The software works, but branch managers ignore it because they lose autonomy over ordering. This is not just a training problem: the system changes who controls a decision.

> [!DEFINITION]
> The **technical view** treats an organization as a formal structure that transforms inputs into outputs. The **behavioral view** emphasizes relationships, rights, obligations, privileges, values, and the compromises through which people work together.

The technical view helps describe resources and production. The behavioral view helps explain why a seemingly efficient redesign meets resistance. Use both: a process diagram can show a new approval route, while a behavioral explanation shows who loses influence under it.

**Organization → system design → changed information access and work → organizational response → revised design.** This is a reciprocal relationship. Avoid claiming that technology alone dictates the final organization.

### Features to notice before recommending a system

| Feature | What to examine | Why it affects an IS project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routines and processes | Established rules for recurring situations | A new workflow may conflict with habitual work |
| Politics | Groups with different interests and resources | More transparent information can redistribute power |
| Culture | Shared assumptions about what matters | A system may challenge accepted ways of working |
| Environment | Customers, suppliers, regulation, competitors | External change creates pressure and uncertainty |
| Structure | Hierarchy, specialization, coordination | Different organizations need different decision support |
| Goals and leadership | Priorities, incentives, styles of control | The same tool may serve different purposes |

The textbook's structure discussion includes entrepreneurial structures, machine bureaucracies, divisionalized bureaucracies, professional bureaucracies, and adhocracies. Compare a small owner-led café with a large standardized chain or a professional organization. Do not assume one reporting structure fits all of them.

## 2. Follow the cost that technology changes

Read §3.3 carefully. Two theories are easy to mix up because both involve coordination.

| Theory | Main question | Costs concerned | Possible IS effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transaction cost theory | Should we obtain work through the market or inside the firm? | Finding partners, negotiating, communicating, monitoring contracts | Lower external coordination costs can make outsourcing more attractive |
| Agency theory | How can a principal ensure agents act appropriately? | Monitoring, coordination, and managing diverging interests | Better information can reduce internal supervision costs |

In the café, comparing suppliers and monitoring delivery agreements concerns **transaction costs**. Giving regional managers reliable branch records to supervise operations concerns **agency costs**. Buying the coffee itself is a production/input expense, not the entire transaction-cost concept.

IT can support flatter hierarchies by giving frontline workers information and letting managers oversee more people. But this is a possible organizational effect, not a guarantee. Firms must still choose decision rights, controls, and incentives. Lower costs do not prove that every business will shrink or decentralize.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q1. Classify the main cost reduced: (a) a portal compares supplier offers and tracks contract fulfillment; (b) a dashboard lets one manager supervise more branches. Explain why the distinction matters.

> [!ANSWER]
> (a) Primarily **transaction costs**, associated with using and coordinating through the market. (b) Primarily **agency costs**, associated with internal monitoring and coordination. The distinction identifies the mechanism; calling both “lower operating cost” misses the causal explanation.

### Resistance is evidence to investigate

A new system may change tasks, structure, technology, and people simultaneously. If managers lose discretion, staff gain extra recording work, or experienced employees lose status, resistance may be understandable. Ask what work and incentives must change, and involve affected groups. Do not explain every failure as employees being irrational or unwilling to learn.

## 3. Diagnose pressure with Porter's five forces

Read §3.4 and inspect its competitive-forces diagram. The model asks **what affects industry competition and the firm's position**, beyond its direct rivals.

| Force | Diagnostic question | Café application, invented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing competitors | How intense is rivalry among established firms? | Nearby cafés compete on price, speed, and taste |
| New entrants | How easy is it to enter? | New sellers can reach customers through delivery platforms |
| Substitutes | What different offering meets the same need? | Packed lunches or convenience-store meals replace café lunch |
| Customers | Can buyers compare, negotiate, or switch easily? | Price comparison and low switching effort increase pressure |
| Suppliers | How dependent is the firm on particular sources? | A unique ingredient with few suppliers limits alternatives |

A **substitute** is not simply another brand of the same product. It satisfies a similar need differently. **Switching costs** are the effort, expense, or loss associated with moving to another provider; they are not identical to a product's price.

### Match a response to the pressure

| IS-supported response | How it works | What to explain in a case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-cost leadership | Improve operating efficiency | Which activity becomes cheaper without undermining required quality? |
| Product differentiation | Offer valued features or experience | Why would customers prefer this offering? |
| Focus on a market niche | Use information to serve a specific segment | Which segment has a distinct need? |
| Customer/supplier intimacy | Strengthen relationships and coordination | What useful integration makes relationships harder to replace? |

The Internet can lower entry and search costs, increase price transparency, and expand rivalry. It can also create new markets and services. State **which force changes, through which mechanism, for which actor**; “the Internet increases competition” alone is incomplete.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q2. The café launches a service tailored to students with a short lunch break. Customers can rely on an exact pickup window. Name a strategy and explain why an app alone is not sufficient.

> [!ANSWER]
> It can combine **niche focus** with **differentiation**. The segment values time reliability. The advantage requires accurate capacity information, preparation routines, and dependable handoff; a copied app interface does not deliver those capabilities by itself.

## 4. Locate the intervention with the value chain

Five forces identifies pressures. The **value chain** identifies activities where changes may improve cost or customer value. Use the models in sequence instead of treating them as competing answers.

Inspect **Figure 3.9** (book p.90 / PDF 119). Primary activities directly contribute to producing and delivering the offering; support activities enable that work.

| Primary activity | Café application | Possible system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inbound logistics | Receive and store ingredients | Inventory and receiving records |
| Operations | Prepare food | Production scheduling and quality tracking |
| Outbound logistics | Dispatch or hand over orders | Pickup/delivery scheduling |
| Sales and marketing | Reach customers and accept orders | Ordering and campaign systems |
| Service | Handle questions and complaints | Customer service records |

**Support activities:** administration/management, human resources, procurement, and technology development. Procurement supports purchasing decisions; inbound logistics handles receiving and storing what arrives. They are related but not identical.

**Benchmarking** compares performance against standards or other organizations. **Best practices** are effective methods identified from experience. Adopting a practice still requires checking whether it fits this firm's context.

A **value web**, shown in **Figure 3.10** (book p.92 / PDF 121), connects partners' value chains so they can coordinate dynamically. It is a network of cooperating firms and activities, not merely a longer internal chain.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q3. Which model should you start with for (a) supplier bargaining pressure, (b) delays in warehouse receiving, and (c) coordinating several partners' value-adding activities?

> [!ANSWER]
> (a) **Five forces** diagnoses external competitive pressure. (b) The **value chain** locates an internal activity and possible improvement. (c) A **value web** examines connected partner activities. A complete recommendation may use all three in sequence.

## 5. Go beyond a single business unit

Read §3.6 and ask where the extra value comes from.

| Concept | Mechanism | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synergy | Units share inputs, outputs, markets, or expertise | Value comes from combining operations |
| Core competency | A deeply developed activity in which the firm excels | Value comes from accumulated expertise, supported by knowledge sharing |
| Network economics | Participation makes a network more valuable | Value comes from useful connections, not just cheaper production |
| Virtual company | Networks coordinate capabilities across firm boundaries | The firm can use assets without owning them all |
| Business ecosystem | Multiple firms or industries jointly deliver value around platforms | Includes complementary roles and often a keystone platform firm |

A growing messaging network gives users more people to reach. That is a network effect. Buying ingredients in bulk at a discount is an economy of scale, not automatically a network effect. More users do not guarantee quality: congestion, poor matching, or harmful interactions can offset benefits.

Link this section back to Chapter 2B: knowledge sharing supports core competencies; coordinating partners depends on reliable information and governance.

## 6. AI changes possibilities, not the need for strategy

Read Tables **3.6–3.7**, book pp.95–96 / PDF 124–125. AI can support forecasting, quality inspection, recommendations, service, logistics, and recruitment. For each application, identify the value-chain activity and the intended business outcome.

AI can also alter competitive forces in different directions. Data, expertise, and computing requirements may raise entry barriers, while available platforms may lower some barriers. Better customer experiences may encourage loyalty, while new comparison tools may strengthen buyers. Explain the particular mechanism rather than claiming one universal effect.

> [!ADVANCED]
> To evaluate an AI proposal, connect **task → data → prediction or generated output → human/organizational action → result**. Then ask what a rival can copy and what depends on distinctive data, knowledge, or execution. “Uses AI” is not a sufficient explanation of advantage.

### Sustainability and alignment

Read §3.8. A strategic system can be expensive, hard to implement, and easy to imitate. **Alignment** means connecting IT choices to business goals and the organizational changes needed to achieve them. Sustainable advantage requires ongoing adaptation, not a one-time purchase.

A useful answer checks three levels: **industry pressure**, **value-adding activities**, and **organizational readiness**. It also names a metric and a risk. For the café's pickup promise, track late orders and customer retention while checking whether kitchen overload makes the promise unrealistic.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Why do technical and behavioral views both matter?
> A: One explains production and formal structure; the other explains interests, rights, and relationships that affect implementation.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Network effects versus economies of scale?
> A: Network effects increase value through participation and connections; economies of scale reduce average cost through greater production or purchasing scale.

> [!FLASHCARD]
> Q: Chapter 3 — Why might an IS advantage disappear?
> A: Rivals can imitate it, customer needs can change, implementation may fail, or the business model may not cover its costs.

> [!QUIZ]
> 3 Q4. Recommend a strategy for the café chain in six linked steps: force, response, activity, system, organizational condition, and durability risk.

> [!ANSWER]
> Example: Customers can switch easily. Differentiate through dependable short-window pickup. Improve operations and outbound handoff. Integrate ordering, capacity, and preparation data. Give staff clear capacity limits and authority to stop accepting impossible slots. Rivals can copy the interface, so the firm must sustain reliable execution and improve the service rather than rely on the app alone.
