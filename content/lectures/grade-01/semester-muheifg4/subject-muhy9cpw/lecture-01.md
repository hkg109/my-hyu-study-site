## Chapter Overview

Information systems have become part of the basic infrastructure of modern business. Organizations use them to coordinate global operations, digitize relationships with customers and suppliers, improve decisions, create new products and services, and redesign work. Technology alone, however, does not guarantee better performance. Value emerges when technology is combined with suitable business processes, skilled people, supportive management, and an organizational culture that can use the new capabilities effectively.

The chapter therefore treats an information system as a sociotechnical system. Hardware, software, data, and networks form the technical side, while people, structure, processes, culture, leadership, and strategy form the social and organizational side.

## 1. How Information Systems Are Transforming Business

Major developments include:

- Greater use of cloud computing, big data, the Internet of Things, mobile platforms, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and generative AI.
- Growth in digital advertising, e-commerce, and online services.
- Wider use of social networking and collaboration platforms inside organizations.
- More flexible organizational structures with less reliance on rigid hierarchy.
- Increased emphasis on employee skills, cross-functional work, and collaborative decision-making.
- The spread of remote and hybrid work supported by digital communication and coordination tools.

Organizations spend heavily on information technology and related services, but successful adoption often requires parallel organizational, managerial, and cultural change.

### Globalization and the Flattened World

Internet-based communication has changed where and how firms operate. Organizations can coordinate production, services, knowledge, and labor across national borders. This creates access to new markets and talent, but it also intensifies competition and requires new skills, processes, and management practices.

### The Emerging Digital Firm

In a digital firm:

- Important relationships with customers, suppliers, and employees are digitally enabled.
- Core business processes are carried out through digital networks.
- Key corporate assets are managed digitally.
- Managers can sense and respond to changes more quickly.
- Time shifting and space shifting make work less dependent on a fixed schedule or location.

## 2. Strategic Business Objectives of Information Systems

Firms invest in information systems to pursue six major objectives.

### 2.1 Operational Excellence

Information systems improve efficiency and productivity by reducing cost, accelerating work, standardizing processes, and providing timely operational information. Walmart illustrates how integrated data and supply-chain systems can support high-volume, low-cost operations.

### 2.2 New Products, Services, and Business Models

Digital technology enables organizations to create offerings and ways of doing business that would otherwise be impossible. A business model explains how a firm produces, delivers, and sells products or services to create value.

### 2.3 Customer and Supplier Intimacy

Better knowledge of customers supports personalized service and repeat business. Close digital coordination with suppliers improves availability, lowers costs, and makes replenishment more responsive.

### 2.4 Improved Decision-Making

Accurate and timely information reduces dependence on guesswork. It can limit overproduction, underproduction, poor resource allocation, and slow responses to changing conditions.

### 2.5 Competitive Advantage

Information systems can help a firm offer better products, lower prices, faster service, stronger supplier relationships, or more responsive customer experiences. Competitive advantage often results from combining several of the other strategic objectives.

### 2.6 Survival

Some systems are required simply to remain in business. Competitive pressure, industry standards, regulation, and customer expectations can make technology adoption a necessity rather than an optional investment.

## 3. What Is an Information System?

### Information Technology and Information Systems

- **Information technology (IT):** the hardware and software a business uses to achieve its objectives.
- **Information system (IS):** a set of interrelated components that collects, processes, stores, and distributes information to support decision-making, coordination, control, analysis, and visualization.

An information system is broader than a computer or software program because it also includes people, procedures, organizational relationships, and managerial purposes.

### Data and Information

- **Data:** raw facts representing events or conditions.
- **Information:** data that has been organized and interpreted so that it becomes meaningful and useful.

For example, individual supermarket checkout records are data. Aggregating them to show total sales by product, store, or territory creates information.

### Core Activities

1. **Input:** captures raw data from inside the organization or its environment.
2. **Processing:** converts data into a meaningful form through classification, arrangement, calculation, or analysis.
3. **Output:** distributes the processed information to people or activities that need it.
4. **Feedback:** returns output to appropriate members of the organization so they can evaluate or correct input and processing.

The organization operates within an environment that includes customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, shareholders, and other external actors.

## 4. The Three Dimensions of Information Systems

### 4.1 Organization

Important organizational elements include:

- People and skills
- Organizational structure and hierarchy
- Business functions
- Business processes
- Culture
- Politics and competing interests

Typical business functions include sales and marketing, manufacturing and production, finance and accounting, and human resources. Organizations also contain senior, middle, and operational management levels, plus knowledge and data workers.

### 4.2 Management

Managers set strategy, allocate resources, respond to business challenges, and coordinate people. They must also act creatively by introducing new products and services or redesigning the organization when existing arrangements no longer fit its environment.

### 4.3 Technology

The technology dimension includes:

- Computer hardware
- Software
- Data management technology
- Networking and telecommunications technology
- The Internet, intranets, extranets, and the World Wide Web

Together, these technologies form the organization's IT infrastructure, which provides a platform for its information systems.

## 5. A Business Perspective on Information Systems

An information system is an organizational and managerial solution built with information technology to address a challenge in the environment. From this perspective, an information system is an instrument for creating value rather than merely a technical installation.

### The Business Information Value Chain

Raw data moves through value-adding activities:

1. Data collection and storage
2. Transformation into useful information
3. Dissemination to relevant users
4. Use in business processes such as supply-chain, enterprise, customer, and knowledge management
5. Use in management activities such as planning, coordination, control, modeling, and decision-making
6. Improved profitability and strategic position

The value of an information system depends partly on whether the organization can use its outputs to improve decisions, processes, and performance.

## 6. Complementary Assets

Technology investment does not automatically produce high returns. Firms receive greater value when they also invest in complementary assets.

### Organizational Assets

- Supportive culture that values efficiency and effectiveness
- Appropriate business models
- Efficient business processes
- Decentralized authority where suitable
- Strong information systems development teams

### Managerial Assets

- Strong senior-management support
- Incentives for innovation
- Teamwork and collaboration
- Training and management development
- A culture that supports flexibility and knowledge-based decisions

### Social Assets

- Internet and telecommunications infrastructure
- Technology standards
- Education and digital literacy
- Laws, regulations, and market institutions that support digital business
- Related service and technology industries

## 7. Approaches to Information Systems

### Technical Approach

The technical approach emphasizes formal models and quantitative methods. It draws from computer science, management science, and operations research.

### Behavioral Approach

The behavioral approach examines issues such as implementation, organizational change, strategy, management, and human behavior. It draws from psychology, sociology, and economics.

### Sociotechnical Approach

The sociotechnical perspective combines technical and behavioral insights. It argues that strong performance comes from jointly optimizing the social system and the technical system. Changing only the technology often produces a suboptimal result because the surrounding jobs, processes, incentives, structure, and culture remain unchanged.

## 8. Cases and Applications

### Walmart's AI-Enabled Supply Chain

Walmart uses a unified inventory view, predictive AI, automation, and agentic tools to match local demand, reduce waste, coordinate stores and fulfillment centers, and improve supplier coordination. The case shows that value depends on decisions about standardization, process redesign, training, shared data, accountability, and technology integration.

### Remote Work

The remote-work case asks students to examine management, organizational, and technological issues created by large-scale work from home. Technology makes remote coordination possible, but performance also depends on work design, culture, supervision, communication, security, and employee needs.

### JurongHealth Services

The healthcare case demonstrates how technologies such as mobile tools and RFID can improve the flow of people, equipment, and information. It also illustrates that successful healthcare systems must fit clinical processes and organizational goals.

## 11. Career Relevance

Information systems knowledge helps professionals understand how data, technology, processes, and people interact. It supports careers in analytics, operations, finance, sales, consulting, product management, and technology-enabled service. Employers value candidates who can translate business needs into system requirements, communicate with technical and nontechnical colleagues, and recognize that successful systems require organizational change as well as technical competence.

## Core Takeaway

Information systems create business value when organizations align technology with strategy, management, processes, people, and culture. The central lesson is not simply to acquire better technology, but to design a better sociotechnical system around it.
