### Slides 1–5 · route, edition change, and the Walmart case

#### Read the Walmart case through management, organization, and technology

- **Problem:** match local demand, stock, waste, stores, fulfillment, and online channels.
- **Technology:** unified inventory + predictive AI + automation + agentic tools.
- **Organization:** redesign replenishment, train staff, share data, align suppliers.
- **Management:** set standardization, AI authority, exception, and accountability rules.
- **Outcome:** availability and decision speed ↑; waste and delay ↓.

> [!CAUTION]
> “The company adopted AI” is not a complete answer. State **which data changes which decision and who owns exceptions and outcomes**.

#### Edition changes and learning questions

The deck opens with video cases on Facebook/eBay data centers, UPS's DIAD and Worldport, and IBM's Raleigh data center. Its four questions are: why IS transforms business; what an IS is and how management, organization, technology, and complementary assets create value; which academic disciplines study IS; and how MIS helps a career. The 18th-edition update makes IoT, big data, cloud, AI, machine learning, and generative AI more explicit and broadens the link to work, skills, globalization, and careers, while retaining sociotechnical thinking.

Walmart's current case starts with a business problem, not an AI label. Local demand, waste, stores, fulfillment centers, and online channels must be coordinated. A unified inventory view, predictive AI, automation, and agentic tools recommend actions. Management decides what to standardize, what AI may support, and who remains accountable; the organization redesigns replenishment, trains associates, shares data, and aligns suppliers; the technology integrates data, models, mobile tools, and a common stack. The expected value is better availability, faster decisions, simpler work, and supplier coordination. The exam question is which of these three dimensions can become the limiting constraint.

### Slides 6–15 · transformation, globalization, and the digital firm

#### Why the digital firm emerged

- **Evidence of change:** online transactions, mobile/social channels, retention rules, IT investment, AI use.
- **Interpretation:** historical numbers are period evidence; the stable conclusion is `investment ≠ value`.
- **Globalization:** communication/transaction cost ↓; access and competition ↑; interdependence ↑.
- **Digital-firm test:** key relationships, processes, and assets are digitally enabled.
- **Time/space shifting:** expands when and where work occurs while increasing coordination needs.

> [!EXAM]
> Digitizing a document is not necessarily digital transformation. Explain how the **process, decision, role, or value proposition** changes.

#### Evidence of business transformation

The deck's historical indicators show why IS became a managerial issue: worldwide websites and online purchasing expanded, social and mobile channels grew, regulation increased data retention, and IT/IT-service spending reached a very large scale. It also gives newer workforce indicators: in 2024, 78% of surveyed organizations reported AI use in at least one function, 71% reported regular generative-AI use, and 39% of workers' core skills were expected to change by 2030. Treat the older market figures as the deck's period evidence, not timeless current statistics. The conclusion is stable: investment creates value only with organizational, managerial, and cultural change.

Figure 1.1 shows IT capital—hardware, software, and communications equipment—rising from about 21% of invested US business capital in 1999 to about 33% in 2017, despite fluctuations in total investment. The next slides connect cloud, big data, IoT, mobile platforms, AI/ML, and social networks to streaming and on-demand business models, mobile/social commerce, BI, virtual meetings, data-based decisions, flatter collaboration, and more direct customer interaction. The remote-work case asks for a management-organization-technology diagnosis, the technologies used, evidence of success, and a reasoned view of remote work and learning.

Global networks lower cross-border operating and transaction costs while increasing competition for jobs, markets, resources, and ideas. They create both dependence and opportunity and require knowledge of different skills and markets. A digital firm digitally enables major stakeholder relationships, performs core processes through digital networks, and manages key assets digitally. Time shifting and space shifting make continuous, distributed global work possible.

### Slides 16–24 · six reasons firms invest

#### Six-objective memory table

| Objective | Result to name | Deck example | Avoid this confusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Operational excellence | Less cost, time, error | Walmart Retail Link | Efficiency alone is not competitive advantage |
| New offerings/models | New value or revenue logic | Apple/iTunes | An app is technology; a model explains delivery and revenue |
| Customer/supplier intimacy | Retention and coordination | Hotels, JCPenney–TAL | Separate customer and supplier effects |
| Improved decisions | Timely evidence replaces guesses | Verizon dashboard | A report matters only when action changes |
| Competitive advantage | Better relative performance | Apple, Walmart, UPS | Name the rival or comparison |
| Survival | Industry or regulatory necessity | ATM, record retention | Mandatory need not be distinctive |

> [!EXAM]
> Use the answer chain `technology → changed process/decision → measurable result → primary objective`. A case may support several objectives; identify the primary one.

#### Business capability and system capability shape each other

Figure 1.2 makes the relationship two-way: strategy, rules, and processes demand changes in hardware, software, databases, and telecommunications, while system capability constrains what the firm can do. The six objectives then appear in a fixed order.

1. **Operational excellence:** efficiency and productivity can raise profits. Walmart's Retail Link joins stores and suppliers to replenish efficiently.
2. **New products, services, and business models:** a business model explains how value is produced, delivered, sold, and monetized. Apple's iTunes and devices changed music distribution and consumption.
3. **Customer and supplier intimacy:** remembered guest preferences can strengthen hotel relationships; JCPenney and TAL Apparel illustrate supplier coordination that bypasses warehouses and reduces inventory.
4. **Improved decision making:** inaccurate or late information causes over/underproduction, misallocated resources, delay, cost, and lost customers. Verizon's dashboard illustrates timely operational visibility.
5. **Competitive advantage:** lower cost, better products, or faster customer/supplier response can build on the previous four objectives; advantage is relative to rivals.
6. **Survival:** ATMs became an industry necessity, while regulation such as Sarbanes-Oxley created record-retention requirements. A survival investment can be mandatory even when it is not unique.

### Slides 25–37 · how an information system works

#### IT and IS differ in scope

| | Information technology · IT | Information system · IS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Technical tools used to process information | The complete arrangement that uses information to perform organizational goals |
| Includes | Hardware, software, storage, networks | IT + data + people + rules + roles + business processes |
| Main question | Which technology is used? | Who uses which information to make which decision and change which work? |
| Success test | Does the tool function? | Do decisions, coordination, control, and performance improve? |

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Buying a tablet and an inventory app is an **IT investment**. Staff recording sales, managers revising orders from reports, and the organization checking later waste form an **information system**.

The deck's house analogy makes the boundary memorable: computers and programs are the lumber, bricks, and tools. A usable house also needs design, work sequence, assigned roles, and a purpose.

#### Data becomes information through purpose and context

| Data | Information |
| --- | --- |
| Raw recorded observations | Data classified, calculated, and summarized for a purpose |
| `item 1042`, `$4.50`, `12:03`, `store A` | `48 sandwiches sold before noon at store A` |
| Limited meaning without context | Supports comparison, judgment, and action |

Figure 1.3 shows the transformation:

1. Capture item number, description, price, quantity, time, and sales location.
2. Group records by product, period, or territory.
3. Calculate units sold, accumulated revenue, and location results.
4. Use the result in inventory, promotion, or purchasing decisions.

==yellow:More data does not automatically mean more information. A decision purpose and valid processing rule give the data meaning.==

#### Four activities form a loop

`Real event → input → processing → output → action → feedback → revised input/process`

| Activity | Function | Checkout example | Design failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Input | Capture raw events inside or outside the firm | Barcode, quantity, price, time, store | Missing, duplicate, or incorrect records |
| Processing | Classify, arrange, calculate, summarize | Sales by hour, item, location | A misleading group or average |
| Output | Deliver processed information to a user or activity | Stock alert, report, dashboard | Too late or too complex to use |
| Feedback | Use results to evaluate and correct the system | Correct a transaction or ordering rule | The same error and decision repeat |

> [!CAUTION]
> Output is not feedback. Printing the report again is more output. Using it to correct missing transactions or revise a forecast is feedback.

#### The system crosses the organizational boundary

- **Customers** supply orders, preferences, and complaints and experience the result.
- **Suppliers** exchange price, inventory, and delivery information.
- **Competitors** change the reference point for product, price, and service decisions.
- **Stockholders** demand performance and risk information.
- **Regulators** define reporting and retention requirements and consume submitted information.

#### Three dimensions explain why the same technology produces different results

##### Organization

Organization supplies the work structure and social setting: senior/middle/operational levels; knowledge, data, and production/service workers; sales/marketing, manufacturing/production, finance/accounting, and HR functions; routines, culture, and politics.

##### Management

Management interprets problems and opportunities, sets objectives, allocates resources, creates offerings and business models, changes authority and performance measures, and assigns accountability for exceptions and outcomes.

##### Technology

Technology supplies hardware, software, data management, networks and telecommunications, and the shared IT infrastructure. The Internet is the network foundation; the Web is one service using it. An intranet limits access to employees, while an extranet extends selected access to authorized partners.

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Information-systems literacy** means understanding how technology, organization, and management interact to produce success or failure—not merely knowing how to operate a computer.

#### Apply the model to JurongHealth

1. Identify the operational or patient-care problem.
2. Name the RFID, mobile, network, or data technology used.
3. Trace input, processing, and output for one system.
4. Explain the changed role, handoff, or clinical workflow.
5. Evaluate waiting time, safety, equipment use, error, and workload.

> [!EXAM]
> Write `technology → input → processing → output → user action → organizational result`. “RFID improves efficiency” skips the information flow and work change that create the result.

### Slides 38–50 · value, complementary assets, and sociotechnical fit

#### Conditions that turn an IT investment into value

`Raw data → value-added information → better decision/process → organizational performance`

- **Organizational assets:** business model, efficient process, collaborative culture.
- **Managerial assets:** leadership, training, incentives, authority, accountability, teamwork.
- **Social assets:** infrastructure, standards, education, institutions, partner ecosystem.
- **Technical approach:** models and efficiency from computer/management science and operations research.
- **Behavioral approach:** people, implementation, and institutions from psychology, sociology, economics.
- **Sociotechnical approach:** jointly adjust both sides; do not choose one and ignore the other.

> [!EXAM]
> Diagnose `missing technical capability` separately from `missing complementary asset`. Good software without authority or training is unused; good culture without needed functionality cannot produce the result.

#### Information value chain and complementary assets

Figures 1.7–1.8 treat IS as a business information value chain: acquire data, transform and distribute information, improve decisions and processes, and seek better performance, profitability, strategic position, or required reporting. Returns vary widely. The technology purchase alone does not select the right model, process, incentives, skills, or standards.

Complementary assets supply that missing capacity. Organizational assets include an appropriate business model and efficient processes; managerial assets include incentives for innovation, teamwork, and collaborative work; social assets include Internet/telecommunications infrastructure and technical standards supplied by society, markets, and government. The highway, fuel, and repair ecosystem that complements a car is the deck's analogy.

The technical approach contributes computer science, management science, operations research, and formal models. The behavioral approach contributes psychology, economics, and sociology to questions such as implementation and strategic integration. MIS combines them with practical business concerns and four actors: technology suppliers, firms, managers/employees, and the legal-social-cultural environment. Figures 1.9–1.10 show the sociotechnical conclusion: jointly adjust organization and technology until a satisfactory performance fit is reached; an advanced tool is not automatically the best fit, and enterprise software may require substantial organizational change. The final career slide applies these ideas to a financial client-support and sales-assistant role at Power Financial Analytics Data Services.

## 1. Begin with a problem, not a device

Imagine a campus café that sells out of sandwiches at noon while throwing away unsold salads. Buying a tablet is easy; deciding what should change is harder. The business needs to learn what sells, when demand changes, and how to adjust purchasing. We will use this **invented café example** throughout the notes.

The opening case in the 18th edition concerns **ConocoPhillips** and digital technology in energy operations. The 17th edition opens with **smart stores**. The settings differ, but both invite the same analysis: identify a business challenge, trace what information becomes available, and explain how work changes. Avoid treating the companies as interchangeable examples from one edition.

### Why the environment pushes firms to change

Mobile access, cloud services, large datasets, connected devices, and AI change both what firms can do and what customers expect. Global networks reduce communication and coordination costs across borders. Firms gain access to suppliers and markets, while facing wider competition and demands for new employee skills.

A **digital firm** goes further than putting an existing form online. Significant relationships with customers, suppliers, and employees are digitally supported; core processes operate through digital networks; important corporate assets are managed digitally. **Time shifting** allows work beyond a fixed business day, and **space shifting** allows work across locations. These are possibilities that require coordination, not promises that every job becomes remote.

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Digital transformation** changes how a firm operates and creates value through digital capabilities. A digital copy of a paper record can contribute, but changing the record format alone does not establish organizational transformation.

**Transition:** once a business can change, it still needs to decide *why* a particular investment is worth making.

## 2. Name the objective before choosing the system

Read §1.4, then attach each objective to a result you could observe.

| Objective | Meaning | Café application: evidence of value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational excellence | Improve efficiency and productivity | Less waste and shorter queues |
| New products, services, and business models | Offer new value or a new way of earning revenue | A prepaid subscription with scheduled pickup |
| Customer and supplier intimacy | Understand and coordinate with partners better | Remember preferences; share replenishment needs |
| Improved decision making | Use timely, relevant information instead of guesses | Adjust tomorrow's order using demand patterns |
| Competitive advantage | Perform better than rivals in ways customers value | Reliable fast service attracts repeat visits |
| Survival | Meet essential competitive or institutional requirements | Support a payment method customers now expect |

**A business model** describes how the firm produces, delivers, and sells its offering to generate revenue. An app is a technology; a subscription is part of a revenue and delivery model. Do not substitute one for the other.

The 18th edition also explicitly discusses **environmental impact, sustainability, governance, and responsible AI** in §1.4. Retain the six named objectives, then explain these additional concerns. For example, measuring waste can support both operational efficiency and sustainability.

> [!QUIZ]
> Q1. Two cafés introduce mobile ordering. One wants to remain acceptable to customers; the other combines it with a uniquely reliable pickup service. Are their strategic objectives necessarily identical?

> [!ANSWER]
> No. The first emphasizes **survival**, while the second may pursue **competitive advantage** through a distinctive service. Both may also improve efficiency. Identify the intended outcome and supporting process, not merely the device or app.

## 3. Follow a record until it changes a decision

Read §§1.5–1.6 and inspect **Figures 1.3–1.5**. In the 18th edition, the data/information example and system-functions diagram are on book p.13, and the three dimensions on p.14. In the 17th edition, start with Figures 1.3–1.5 on book pp.47–49.

> [!DEFINITION]
> An **information system (IS)** coordinates the collection, processing, storage, and distribution of information to support decisions and organizational activity. **Information technology (IT)** supplies the hardware and software tools. The system also depends on the work and decisions surrounding those tools.

| Stage | Café example | What changes? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input | Item, quantity, price, and time from each sale | A real event becomes a record |
| Processing | Group sales by product and hour; compare with stock | Records acquire structure and context |
| Output | A noon stockout report for the manager | Information reaches someone who can act |
| Feedback | Compare recommended purchases with actual waste | Results help correct later input or processing |

**Data** are raw observations; **information** makes observations meaningful for a purpose. “48” alone says little. “48 sandwiches sold before noon, against 30 planned” supports a purchasing decision. Storage preserves records but does not, by itself, make them useful.

==yellow:Business value depends on using information to change decisions or work.== A beautiful report that nobody uses may create little value.

> [!QUIZ]
> Q2. A system calculates daily sales and prints a report. What additional action would make the report part of a feedback loop?

> [!ANSWER]
> Staff use it to identify missing transactions or revise the forecasting process, then check the corrected result. Feedback means returning output to evaluate or correct system activity; printing another copy is only more output.

## 4. Ask three questions about every implementation

Technology is visible, but organizational arrangements determine whether its output can be used.

| Dimension | Ask | Café diagnosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organization | How are work, authority, routines, and culture arranged? | Who records waste? Can shifts share stock information? |
| Management | Which goals, decisions, resources, and changes are required? | Who sets waste targets and approves revised orders? |
| Technology | Which tools collect, process, store, and communicate data? | POS devices, database, reporting software, network |

Organizations have functional specialties—sales/marketing, manufacturing/production, finance/accounting, and human resources—and management levels. Senior managers set long-term direction; middle managers translate and monitor plans; operational managers oversee daily work. Knowledge workers create knowledge, data workers assist information processing, and production/service workers deliver outputs.

The technology platform includes hardware, software, data management, and networking/telecommunications. Distinguish the **Internet**, the network of networks, from the **Web**, a service using it. An **intranet** uses Internet technologies for internal access; an **extranet** extends selected access to authorized outsiders. Chapter 2 will connect these to enterprise processes.

**Computer literacy** concerns using and understanding computing technology. **Information systems literacy** adds organizational, managerial, and behavioral understanding. A person can operate a dashboard without understanding why its incentives cause staff to enter misleading data.

## 5. Connect the technology vocabulary

Read §1.7 as a relationship among capabilities rather than four separate definitions.

**Connected devices → observations → large datasets → computing capacity and analysis → possible action.** This is a learning aid; a real implementation can omit or rearrange parts.

| Capability | Role | Limit to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internet of Things (IoT) | Connected devices sense or exchange operational data | A sensor reading may be missing or inaccurate |
| Big data | Large, varied datasets offer material for analysis | Volume does not guarantee relevance or quality |
| Cloud computing | Remote computing resources support storage and processing | Moving data does not fix a broken process |
| AI and machine learning | Recognize patterns, predict, or perform tasks using computational methods | Predictions need suitable data and evaluation |
| Generative AI | Produces new text, images, or other content | Plausible output is not proof of correctness |

For the café, a temperature sensor can produce records; cloud services can store them; an analysis system can flag unusual patterns; staff decide whether to inspect a refrigerator. Explain the complete path before claiming that “AI solves the problem.”

## 6. Explain why the same software produces different results

Read §§1.8–1.9. **Complementary assets** are investments and conditions that allow another investment to create value. A new system and the organizational changes around it work together.

| Asset group | Examples | Failure if absent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organizational | Efficient processes, suitable business model, supportive culture | Staff enter data twice or ignore shared records |
| Managerial | Leadership support, training, incentives, teamwork | Nobody owns the new decision or rewards useful behavior |
| Social | Education, networks, standards, supportive institutions | Partners cannot exchange or interpret information reliably |

The **technical approach** draws on computer science, management science, and operations research. The **behavioral approach** draws on psychology, sociology, and economics. The **sociotechnical view** seeks a workable fit between the technical and social parts, adjusting both where needed.

> [!CAUTION]
> Buying the technically strongest product is not automatically the best organizational solution. Likewise, training alone cannot repair missing functionality. Evaluate both sides together.

> [!QUIZ]
> Q3. A chain installs identical forecasting software at every branch. Only branches that train staff and change ordering authority improve. Explain the result without claiming that the software is irrelevant.

> [!ANSWER]
> The software provides a useful capability, but complementary managerial and organizational assets let branches use it. Training supports correct interpretation; changed authority lets employees act. This is a sociotechnical explanation: neither technology alone nor organization alone explains the outcome.
